# Project Hail Mary · Reading Notes Chapter 1

Finished Chapter 1 tonight. The whole chapter is one continuous scene: a man wakes up in a medical bay with no memory, a computer keeps asking him `What's two plus two?`, and he slowly re-learns that he has a body. That is the chapter. Notes below.

## Plot summary

- The protagonist — unnamed, amnesiac — wakes up blind, deaf-ish, unable to move, hooked to a breathing mask.
- A calm, feminine, computer voice keeps repeating: `What's two plus two?` He is too tired to care, drifts back to sleep, gets asked again.
- He gradually recovers control: first eyelids, then mouth, then a single hand. After several failed attempts he croaks `Fffoouurr` — the computer accepts it. First win.
- He is lying on an oval bed mounted to a wall. Cameras watch him. Two robotic arms with surgical-looking tools hang from the ceiling. He is naked, full of tubes, and has no idea who he is.
- Last question of the chapter: `What's your name?` He doesn't know.

## Vocabulary & language

Words I had to look up or think about from the chapter:

- **irritate** — *annoy* in a slightly stronger, more active sense. The chapter uses it for the question nagging him back to consciousness.
- **drift back (to sleep)** — to slide back into sleep without deciding to. `drift` carries the "not really choosing" feeling that `fall back` does not.
- **feminine** — here purely descriptive of the computer's voice (音色). Not the same as `female` (biological) or `womanly` (气质).
- **hassle** — *annoy/bother*, but with a colloquial, slightly complaining tone. Implies the other party won't stop. Fits Grace's voice better than `annoy` would.
- **wiggle** — small, slightly clumsy movement. `I felt a wiggle that time` is funnier and more vivid than `I felt a movement`.
- **eyelid** — pure Germanic compound (eye + lid). Most English anatomy words this everyday have a Latin/Greek pair (palpebra) that lives in medical jargon; the everyday word stays descriptive. Will probably come back to this in a later chapter.

Grammar point that came up:

- `I don't have much to work with.` — `much` is the direct object of `have`. `to work with` is an infinitive phrase modifying `much`, equivalent to a reduced relative clause `much [that I can work with]`. The infinitive rides on the noun, not the verb.

## On the opening line

Conventional opening sentences do one of three things: set a scene, name the weather, or drop a hook. Weir does none of these. He opens with a math problem a five-year-old could solve.

It works because the question is doing two jobs at once:

1. **In medias res, aggressively.** We don't know who is asking, why, or to whom. The reader is dropped into the same fog the protagonist is in. By the time we find out it's a computer, we have already accepted the rules of the world: this is going to be confusing, and someone is going to have to figure it out slowly.
2. **It sets the register.** A sci-fi novel that starts with a kindergarten question is signalling: this book is going to be patient, curious, and a little funny. If it had opened with a paragraph of orbital mechanics we would expect a different book.

The LitCharts summary of the opening calls it "an intense state of disorientation" — which is the right word, but it leaves out the warmth. Grace's reaction to all this is not dread. It is sleepy irritation, then low-stakes curiosity, then jokes at the computer's expense. Weir is doing what he did in *The Martian*: putting a competent, likeable, slightly nerdy person in a bad situation, and trusting the reader to root for the competence.

## Style notes

- **First person, present tense.** The chapter reads like a live feed from inside Grace's skull. Present tense is the right call here — the past tense would have made the waking-up feel narrated, not experienced.
- **Short sentences for sensory discovery.** When he is cataloguing the room, the rhythm collapses to one-line beats: `A bed.` `Cameras in the ceiling watch my every move.` `Creepy though that is, I'm much more concerned about the robot arms.` The clip mirrors a mind that is taking inventory one object at a time.
- **Humor as a load-bearing structure.** The chapter is otherwise bleak (naked, alone, can't move, can't remember). It stays readable because Grace keeps making small jokes at his own expense. The two robotic arms are `disturbingly penetration-`-looking. The computer is his `tormenter`. This is the *Martian* voice transplanted to a worse starting condition.

## Open questions going into Chapter 2

- Where is "here"? The room is round, plastic, with a hatch instead of a door. Not a hospital on Earth.
- Who is the computer? Medical AI? Ship's computer? Something else?
- Why the amnesia? Drug, trauma, or deliberate?
- The chapter ends with `What's your name?` — does Grace recover it, or does the reader get it later?

Predicting nothing, but I am fairly sure Grace is the only one who makes it out of wherever this is. The first chapter is too clean for there to be other moving characters in it.
