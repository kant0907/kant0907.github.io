# Hillbilly Elegy · Reading Notes Day 1

I haven't started the main text yet. Today's session was a Q&A to get the terminology and background straight. Notes below.

## Hillbilly

- **Etymology**: likely "hill-folk" (Scots-Irish) + the generic name "Billy." First attested in early-1900s Appalachian regional writing. Originally a **self-identifier**, not always a slur — comparable to "redneck" in its reclamation arc.
- **Who it refers to**:
  - **Narrow sense**: descendants of Scots-Irish (Ulster Scots) settlers who moved into Appalachia (and later the Ozarks) from the 18th century on. Vance's family fits this — grandparents moved from Jackson, KY to Middletown, OH for factory work, a classic Rust Belt migration.
  - **Looser sense**: poor rural white Americans generally, including people without Scots-Irish roots. Vance sometimes uses it this way.
- **Two senses the book blends**:
  1. **Ethnic-cultural identity** — shared inheritance: fierce family loyalty, distrust of institutions, honor-based conflict, kin as the only reliable social safety net, fatalism, suspicion of "educated" outsiders.
  2. **Class descriptor** for the white underclass — job loss, opioid addiction, family instability, what Vance calls "adversity passed down like an inheritance."
- **Distinguish from "white trash"**:
  - **Hillbilly** = regional / ethnic-cultural identity
  - **White trash** = class-based slur, applied to poor whites regardless of region
  - Conflating them is one of the things critics keep flagging about the book.

## Elegy

- From Greek *elegeia* — a poem of mourning, traditionally for the dead. An **elegy** laments something already lost.
- The title is doing double duty:
  - **Personal**: for Vance's own family — absent fathers, addicted mother, the grandparents who held things together, the Middletown neighborhood that was already crumbling while he was growing up in it.
  - **Cultural / class**: for the white working class as it used to be — stable factory jobs, two-parent households, church on Sunday, the implicit social contract where hard work led to a decent life. Vance is saying *that world is gone*.
- **How the elegy frame shapes the book**:
  - Bittersweet tone, not angry. The anger is in the social commentary; the family scenes are written with tenderness.
  - Vance positions himself as a mourner, not a judge. He keeps saying "I am one of these people" before he criticizes them.
  - Implied reader: fellow mourners, especially working-class whites who recognize the loss — and implicitly, the rest of America who should mourn with him.
- **Questions to keep open while reading**:
  - Is the elegy honest, or does it skip accountability? ("We lost something" can paper over "we also broke something.")
  - Can you write an elegy for a living culture, or does the form itself overstate the loss?
  - Why does the word *elegiac* fit better than *indignant* for the book's tone?

## Rust Belt

- Informal name for a band of US states — Great Lakes south through the Ohio River Valley into parts of the mid-Atlantic — that was the heart of 20th-century American heavy industry and lost most of it from the 1970s on.
- **Core states**: Ohio, Michigan, Pennsylvania, Indiana, West Virginia, western New York.
- **Vance's two anchor cities**:
  - **Middletown, OH** — classic Rust Belt town: AK Steel plant, decades of layoffs, an opioid crisis that hit the area hard. He uses it as a stand-in for the whole region's decline.
  - **Jackson, KY** — in the Appalachian foothills, where his grandparents came from. Pre-industrial, the kind of place hillbillies left to come to the Rust Belt's factories.
- **Why it "rusted"** (overlapping causes):
  1. Automation
  2. Offshoring (steel to South Korea, autos to Mexico, manufacturing to China)
  3. Trade and policy shifts (1970s deindustrialization, NAFTA, China's 2001 WTO entry)
  4. Union decline
- **Why it matters in the book**: Vance treats the opioid crisis, family breakdown, and "deaths of despair" (Case and Deaton's term) as downstream of the Rust Belt's economic collapse. Hillbillies didn't decay in a vacuum — they decayed because the economy that had welcomed them collapsed.

## Marine Corps (USMC)

- One of the US armed forces branches, distinct from the Army, Navy, Air Force, and Space Force. Founded 1775, smallest of the main service branches (~180,000 active duty).
- **Key cultural elements**:
  - "Every Marine a rifleman" — every role is, in principle, a combat role
  - **Esprit de corps** — very strong internal identity; "you are a Marine forever"
  - 13-week boot camp at Parris Island (SC) or San Diego (CA), famously tough
  - "Semper Fidelis" motto
- **What Vance takes from the Marines**:
  1. **Structure replaces chaos** — fixed wake-up, fixed meals, fixed consequences, vs. his household where rules changed by the hour.
  2. **A new reference group** — for the first time, the people around him are measured by effort and discipline, not by family name.
  3. **Pride in something earned, not inherited** — contrasts hillbilly pride (often defensive, about bloodline) with Marine pride (about what you've done).
- **A caveat**: Vance's account of the Marines is almost entirely positive — no trauma, no moral injury, no friends lost. He served during the Iraq War (2003–2007) but the book skips his deployments almost entirely. The Marines in this book function more as **a narrative device** than as a complete portrait of military experience.

## Enlist / Why the Marines, not the Army or Navy

- **Enlist** = formally sign up for military service (vs. **commission** as an officer, a different path through college + OCS or a service academy).
- **Enlistment process** for someone like Vance:
  1. Take the **ASVAB** — multiple-choice test determining which jobs you qualify for.
  2. Talk to a **recruiter** — a salesperson, often aggressive, especially in working-class towns.
  3. Pick an **MOS** (Military Occupational Specialty) — enlisted Marines don't just sign up "as a Marine"; they sign up for a specific job (infantry, intelligence, logistics, mechanic, etc.).
  4. Ship to **boot camp** — 13 weeks at Parris Island or San Diego.
  5. Graduate, get assigned to a unit.
- **Why Vance enlisting matters**: not just a career move, but the **single decision that changed his life**.
  1. **Escape from family chaos** — leaving Middletown, his mom's boyfriends, the neighborhood.
  2. **Adoption into a new family** — a structural, demanding, hierarchical family that won't let him fall apart.
  3. **The first time an institution invests in him as a person** — not because of who his family is, but because the US government now owns his development.
- **Why the Marines, not Army or Navy**:
  1. **Toughest reputation** — the obvious pick for a working-class kid who wants the hardest thing available.
  2. **Family and community pattern** — Scots-Irish Appalachian and Rust Belt communities disproportionately enlist in the Marines. Tradition + "most demanding" + most "real" or "hard" branch.
  3. **Smaller, tighter, more "in or out" identity** — the smallest branch, with the strongest "you are a Marine forever" bond. The Army is bigger and more bureaucratic.
  4. **Shorter, harder boot camp** — 13 weeks, famous drill instructors, relentless.
  5. **Quick deployments, clear missions** — the Marines are the "911 force" sent in fast.
- **What this reveals about hillbilly culture**:
  - **Toughness as a primary virtue** — pick the hardest option.
  - **Distrust of "soft" institutions** — Air Force and Navy are associated with comfortable postings; Marines are not.
  - **Loyalty to a tight group, not a big system** — Marines are small and tribal in a way the Army isn't.
- **A subtle point**: in everyday English "enlist" and "join" are interchangeable, but in the book Vance always uses **enlist** — military-specific, formal, contractual. The verb itself signals that something weighty is happening, not a casual choice.

## GI Bill

- US federal law that gives military veterans money for college, job training, and a housing allowance.
- **Major versions**:
  - **Post-9/11 GI Bill** (the one Vance used) — full in-state tuition at public universities (or up to a cap at private schools), housing stipend, books and supplies, up to 36 months. Requires 90+ days of active service post-9/11/2001.
  - **Montgomery GI Bill** — older, fixed monthly payment, less generous.
  - **Yellow Ribbon Program** — covers tuition above the cap at expensive private schools (Yale Law is the kind of school that uses this).
- **Why it matters in the book**: the book treats the Marines as the **structure** that broke Vance out, and the **GI Bill as the permanent exit ramp**. The argument underneath is that the military + GI Bill is one of the last reliable upward-mobility paths left for people like him.
- **The full ladder**:
  ```
  Hillbilly childhood (no money, no stability)
      ↓
  Enlist in Marines (structure + discipline)
      ↓
  GI Bill (pays for college)
      ↓
  Ohio State → Yale Law → exit
  ```
  Without the GI Bill, post-Marines Vance is a working-class guy with no degree. With it, he can afford Ohio State, then Yale Law, then a venture capital career in California.

## Venture Capital (VC) Firm

- A **fund**, not a normal company:
  1. The VC firm raises a **fund** — a big pile of money — from **limited partners (LPs)**: pension funds, university endowments, wealthy families, sovereign wealth funds.
  2. The VC firm (the **general partner, GP**) invests that money into **startups** it picks.
  3. The startups the GP picks are the **portfolio**.
  4. When a portfolio company gets sold or IPOs, the GP returns most of the profit to the LPs and keeps a chunk as fee + carried interest.
  5. If most of the portfolio fails — the normal outcome — the LPs absorb the loss.
- **What VCs actually do**:
  - **Source deals** — find founders to invest in.
  - **Pick** — say yes or no to a startup seeking funding.
  - **Wire the money** — typically in rounds: seed, Series A, B, C…
  - **Help the company** — usually a board seat, advice, introductions.
  - **Sell the stake eventually** — through an acquisition (M&A) or IPO, ideally at 10–100× entry.
- **Three things worth knowing**:
  1. The "1 in 10, or worse" math — most VC investments go to zero; a fund is profitable only if one or two winners are huge.
  2. The "pattern matching" job — VCs are professional analogy-makers: "This founder reminds me of the Airbnb founders at the same stage." Recognition, not analysis. Vance's memoir-of-pattern-recognition (recognizing dysfunction, recognizing hillbilly behavior) is almost the same skill, just pointed at a different problem.
  3. It's a small, gossipy world — a few hundred serious VC firms, an inbred network obsessed with "who's hot." This also explains Vance's later political moves (Ohio Senate run, 2024 VP pick) — small elite networks that all know each other.
- **The bit easy to miss**: Vance's framing of the VC job keeps returning to a **gatekeeper question** — *who gets through the door?* In Middletown, it was the Marines. In college, it was the application. In VC, it's the partner meeting. The book is partly about how the people he grew up with **never even reach the door** of the institutions that decide their lives.

## "For Mamaw and Papaw, my very own hillbilly terminator"

- Mamaw (Bonnie) and Papaw (Jim) Vance — the **stable, fierce center** of his childhood. The ones who, in his telling, kept the family from collapsing entirely.
- **Breaking it down**:
  - **Mamaw and Papaw** = his maternal grandparents
  - **Hillbilly** = the cultural identity the book is about
  - **Terminator** = the Schwarzenegger-movie archetype — unkillable, relentless, doesn't stop, doesn't negotiate
  - The sentence says: **his grandparents were his personal, hillbilly-flavored version of the Terminator — the unstoppable force that protected him.**
- **Two readings at once**:
  1. **Literal**: Mamaw in particular was **legitimately intimidating** — a ferocious temper, physically fought to defend family, once threw boiling coffee at an abusive boyfriend. Papaw was a hard worker and a drinker. The "terminator" is not metaphor; Vance is saying they were the **muscle** that stood between him and the chaos of his mother's life.
  2. **Thematic**: the "hillbilly terminator" gives you the book's **cultural theory in miniature** — hillbilly culture at its best: fierce loyalty to kin, zero tolerance for anyone who threatens kin, honor-based aggression, you don't call the police you handle it, relentlessness.
- **Why it's one of the book's signature lines**: Vance uses it at a moment of **affection, not criticism**. Most of the book treats hillbilly culture as a cause of dysfunction; this line is one of the places where Vance admits the same culture has a **heroic, even mythic version** — and that he wouldn't have survived without it.
- **Voice note**: self-deprecating + affectionate + a pop-culture frame to make it land. The Terminator reference tells you he knows it's a slightly absurd comparison, but he means it.
- **The uncomfortable part**: Vance is **glorifying the exact behavior the book elsewhere treats as a problem**. Honor-based aggression, family-as-the-only-valid-institution, "I solve it with force" — these are also the things the book blames for some of the dysfunction. The Mamaw line is a place where Vance openly loves the contradiction, but doesn't fully resolve it.
